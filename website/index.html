Study overview

Accuracy tells us whether an answer is correct. When a problem has several correct answers, we can ask another question: do people and AI models choose the same solutions?

The study covers 100 main puzzles, each with 3–8 correct solution classes, plus 170 puzzles and variants for the presentation and context experiments. Some equivalent forms, such as the same sum written in a different order, are grouped into one solution class.

Our analysis includes 521 human participants and repeated responses from GPT-5.6 Sol, Claude Opus 4.8 and Gemini 3.5 Flash. The figures label these models ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini.

Our main finding: people and models choose different correct answers. Averaged across puzzle families, the models’ solution distributions are closer to one another than to the human distribution. Human choices are also more diverse.

For each puzzle, a solution distribution records the share of correct answers in each solution class. We estimate one distribution for humans, pooling answers across participants, and one for each model, using separately generated responses.

Solution diversity

We measure solution diversity with normalized Shannon entropy. A value of 0 means always choosing the same solution; 1 means choosing uniformly among all valid solutions. Humans have the highest mean entropy in every puzzle family.

These comparisons use correct answers only. Unless stated otherwise, models use the low reasoning-effort setting and plain prompts.

Distribution comparisons

To compare solution distributions directly, we use total-variation distance (TV). A distance of 0 means identical distributions; 1 means no overlap.

Across the main study, mean TV is 0.32–0.38 between models, compared with 0.45–0.54 between humans and models. These are distances between distributions of correct answers, not error rates.

Presentation and context

We kept the valid solutions fixed while changing how puzzles were presented: highlighting cells, reflecting boards, reordering numbers, adding a preceding puzzle or providing a strategy primer.

Highlighting and reflecting boards shift model choices more than human choices. For paired puzzles, the pattern differs: human answers show greater mutual information—a measure of dependence between the two answers—than model answers.

Relative difficulty

Within a puzzle family, problems that take people longer tend to use more model reasoning tokens. Across families, that relationship reverses: Arithmetic has the highest relative effort for humans and the lowest for all three models. Maze requires the most relative effort for models but is below the human baseline.

We use median time for correct human answers and median reasoning-token count for valid model answers as proxies for difficulty. Scores are relative to each source’s own baseline. This analysis was exploratory and was not preregistered.

Prompting and reasoning effort

We compared plain prompts with prompts asking the model to answer as a human participant, at low and medium reasoning-effort settings. These changes alter model answer distributions, but neither intervention consistently brings them closer to human choices.

Study scope and limitations

People and models can both answer correctly while making different choices. Comparing their distributions over valid solutions reveals patterns that an accuracy score alone misses.

The findings describe answer choices and leave the underlying strategies unresolved. They concern small puzzles with fully enumerable solution sets, three model versions and the tested prompt and effort settings. Whether the patterns extend to other tasks remains open.

Study design and scope
  • Participants: 104 in the main study and 417 in the presentation and context study, after exclusions. All were recruited through Prolific.
  • Primary comparison: 100 main puzzles, with 20 per family; AI models use low effort and plain prompts. Families receive equal weight in overall summaries.
  • Answers included: distributions use correct human answers and valid model answers. Percentages are shares of those answers, rather than overall success rates.
  • Collection: humans could receive feedback and retry; each model response was generated once, without feedback. Humans saw paired puzzles sequentially; models saw both together.
  • Uncertainty: intervals reflect variation across puzzles within each family, holding each puzzle’s recorded answers or median effort fixed. They do not model dependence across puzzles answered by the same participant.
  • Scope: U.S. adult participants, the tested model versions and the study’s puzzle, prompt and sampling choices.

Code, data, and citation

The reproduction repository contains the study materials, recorded model responses and code to collect, process and analyze the data and generate figures and tables. Individual human response records are not included.
